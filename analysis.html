Smoking behavior in the U.S. has long been shaped by age and sex, with older adults and men historically more likely to have smoked. Using a 1,000-adult sample from NHANES 2009–2012, we ask: what is the difference in the probability of being an ever-smoker between a 30-year-old woman and a 70-year-old man? We model smoke as a Bernoulli outcome linked via logistic regression to age and sex, so predicted probabilities come from transforming the linear combination of these covariates through the logit link. The model treats a heavy lifetime smoker and someone who tried 100 cigarettes decades ago identically, even though they’re very different from a campaign-targeting perspective. Men have log-odds of being an ever-smoker about 0.54 higher than women, holding nothing else constant — a positive, statistically detectable difference since the interval excludes zero. Each additional year of age is associated with a 0.007 increase in log-odds of being an ever-smoker, but the interval barely crosses zero, so the effect isn’t clearly distinguishable from no effect. Adjusting for age, men have log-odds of being an ever-smoker about 0.545 higher than women — nearly unchanged from the sex-only model, confirming age and sex aren’t confounding each other. Comparing a 70-year-old man to a 30-year-old woman, we estimate a 13.3-percentage-point higher probability of ever having smoked (95% CI: 7.2% to 19.3%). Our estimate could be wrong for several reasons: the data comes from NHANES 2009–2012, while the question asks about the population today — smoking cohort patterns have shifted enough that the age and sex coefficients may no longer hold. The smoke variable also captures lifetime ever-smoker status rather than current smoking, so our model may overstate who a present-day cessation campaign should actually target. If more current data were available, refitting on a recent NHANES cycle would likely produce a smaller estimate, given falling smoking rates nationwide.
